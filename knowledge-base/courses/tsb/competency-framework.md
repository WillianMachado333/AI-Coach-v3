# Understanding Traits, Skills, and Behaviors — Competency framework

> Administrative view: what the course actually teaches, mapped as competencies. Used by coaches and by Erica to ground reflections. Each unit corresponds one-to-one to a lesson in course-content.md.

## Competency 1 · Understanding Traits, Skills, and Behaviors

### FfTT-TSB 1.1 · trait awareness

Traits influence how people tend to think, feel, communicate, and respond. Understanding traits helps people recognize natural tendencies without treating them as fixed excuses or permanent limitations.

### Competency Definition

Understanding stable personal tendencies

### Competency Statement

Recognizes traits as relatively stable patterns that influence how a person tends to think, feel, communicate, and respond across situations.

### Knowledge

- Traits are patterns rather than guarantees.
- Traits often show consistency across time and situations.
- Experience, roles, culture, and context can affect how traits are expressed.
- Trait assessments describe tendencies and should not be treated as fixed labels.

### Skills

- Identify language that describes a tendency.
- Separate a trait from a single action.
- Explain how one trait may appear differently across settings.
- Use trait information without predicting behavior with certainty.

### Observable Behaviors

- Describes a trait using words such as “tends to” or “often.”
- Explains that a person can manage how a trait is expressed.
- Compares how the same tendency may help in one setting and create risk in another.
- Avoids treating an assessment result as a complete description of a person.

### I Demonstrate This When

- I understand that traits describe tendencies, not guarantees.
- I recognize that people can manage how traits show up.
- I avoid expecting training to change someone’s personality quickly.
- I consider how life experience may shape traits over time.

### Performance Indicators

- **Limited**: Needs prompting to demonstrate recognize traits and may apply it inaccurately or inconsistently.
- **Developing**: Names traits but may describe them as fixed.
- **Effective**: Describes traits as tendencies and connects them to possible behavior.
- **Consistent**: Considers context, avoids certainty, and uses trait information to support fair choices.

### Examples

- A learner notes that a preference for careful planning may support quality but may slow action during an urgent task.
- A supervisor uses a work-style assessment to open a discussion rather than to decide what an employee will always do.

### Non-Examples

- Saying, “She is an introvert, so she cannot lead a meeting.”
- Assuming one impatient response proves that impatience is a permanent trait.

### Common Misconceptions

- Misconception: Traits never change. Correction: Traits are relatively stable, but they may shift over time and can be expressed differently.
- Misconception: A trait predicts behavior in every situation. Correction: Skills, goals, pressure, expectations, and context also shape behavior.

### Conversation Starters

- Understanding: How are traits different from choices?
- Reflection or Application: How might one of my tendencies help me in some situations and create risk in others?
- Practice or Improvement: What can I practice so I manage a strong trait more effectively?

### FfTT-TSB 1.2 · skill awareness

Skills are learned capabilities that can improve through practice, feedback, instruction, and experience. Understanding skills helps people focus development efforts on what can be built and strengthened.

### Competency Definition

Understanding developable learned capabilities

### Competency Statement

Recognizes skills as capabilities that can be developed through learning, practice, coaching, feedback, and repeated application.

### Knowledge

- Skills are learned and can improve.
- Knowledge may support a skill, but knowing is not the same as performing.
- Practice, feedback, reflection, and repeated use support skill growth.
- Skills can transfer across tasks, although application may differ by context.

### Skills

- Identify the skill needed for a task.
- Break a broad capability into actions that can be practiced.
- Seek and use feedback.
- Connect skill growth to changes in performance.

### Observable Behaviors

- Names a specific skill instead of a vague personal quality.
- Explains how practice would strengthen the skill.
- Uses feedback to adjust an attempt.
- Applies a skill in more than one relevant situation.

### I Demonstrate This When

- I identify skills I can build through practice.
- I understand that learning a skill takes effort and repetition.
- I use feedback to strengthen my capabilities.
- I connect skill growth to better workplace actions.

### Performance Indicators

- **Limited**: Needs prompting to demonstrate recognize skills and may apply it inaccurately or inconsistently.
- **Developing**: Recognizes that skills can grow but may set broad goals.
- **Effective**: Identifies a specific skill and a practical way to build it.
- **Consistent**: Selects focused practice, uses feedback, and applies the skill across settings.

### Examples

- A new employee practices summarizing customer concerns before offering a solution.
- A student improves presentation skills through rehearsal, feedback, and repeated delivery.

### Non-Examples

- Calling confidence a skill without identifying the actions that build confident performance.
- Reading about active listening but never practicing it in conversation.

### Common Misconceptions

- Misconception: People either have a skill or they do not. Correction: Skill levels can develop through focused effort.
- Misconception: Completing training proves skill. Correction: Skill is shown through effective performance.

### Conversation Starters

- Understanding: What makes a skill different from a trait or a piece of knowledge?
- Reflection or Application: Which skill would help me respond better in a situation I find difficult?
- Practice or Improvement: How can I create a simple practice plan for a skill I want to build?

### FfTT-TSB 1.3 · behavior awareness

Behavior is what a person actually does. It is observable, situation-based, and influenced by traits, skills, expectations, pressure, motivation, relationships, and environment.

### Competency Definition

Recognizing personal patterns and tendencies

### Competency Statement

The ability to recognize personal tendencies, strengths, risks, and recurring patterns in order to make more intentional choices about workplace behavior. Workplace Relevance Employees who understand their own tendencies are better prepared to manage communication, respond to feedback, work with different people, and adapt to changing expectations. Self-awareness supports professionalism, accountability, teamwork, leadership readiness, and continuous improvement.

### Knowledge

- Behavior is observable and situation-based.
- A single behavior does not define the whole person.
- Behavior may change when expectations, stress, support, or incentives change.
- Impact can be discussed without assuming intent.

### Skills

- Describe what a person said or did.
- Separate observation from interpretation.
- Identify relevant context.
- Compare behavior across situations before identifying a pattern.

### Observable Behaviors

- Uses specific action words to describe behavior.
- Distinguishes a fact from an assumption about motive.
- Considers what was happening before and during the action.
- Avoids turning one event into a personality label.

### I Demonstrate This When

- I focus on what I observed someone do.
- I recognize that people may behave differently in different situations.
- I avoid turning one behavior into a broad personality label.
- I consider the situation before drawing conclusions.

### Performance Indicators

- **Limited**: Needs prompting to demonstrate recognize behaviors and may apply it inaccurately or inconsistently.
- **Developing**: Notices actions but mixes them with judgments.
- **Effective**: Describes observable actions and relevant context.
- **Consistent**: Identifies patterns carefully, explains impact, and avoids unsupported conclusions.

### Examples

- A team member says, “He interrupted three times during the discussion,” instead of, “He is disrespectful.”
- A coach compares behavior in routine and high-pressure situations before suggesting practice.

### Non-Examples

- Calling someone lazy because one deadline was missed.
- Treating an intention to help as evidence that the behavior was effective.

### Common Misconceptions

- Misconception: Behavior shows exactly who a person is. Correction: Behavior is evidence about an action in a situation, not a complete identity.
- Misconception: Only visible physical actions count. Correction: Spoken, written, and digital actions can also be observed.

### Conversation Starters

- Understanding: What makes a description of behavior objective rather than judgmental?
- Reflection or Application: How can I separate what I observed from what I assumed in a recent interaction?
- Practice or Improvement: Can you help me rewrite a vague label as a clear behavior statement?
- FfTT-TSB 2: Recognizing Personal Tendencies
- Personal tendencies influence how people prefer to work, communicate, respond to stress, make decisions, and interact with others. Recognizing these tendencies helps learners understand themselves more accurately and make more intentional choices about behavior.
- Problem Statement
- Many people are unaware of their own patterns, which can cause repeated misunderstandings, ineffective habits, or unhelpful reactions at work.
- Value Statement
- Self-awareness helps people use their strengths, manage risks, and choose more effective behaviors in different workplace situations.

## Competency 2 · Recognizing Personal Tendencies

### FfTT-TSB 2.1 · identifying personal patterns

People often repeat patterns in how they communicate, organize work, handle pressure, and respond to others. Recognizing these patterns is an important first step toward professional growth.

### Competency Definition

Identifying recurring personal responses

### Competency Statement

Recognizes recurring patterns in personal communication, work habits, decision-making, emotional responses, and interactions with others.

### Knowledge

- patterns are identified across several situations
- triggers and conditions can make patterns more likely
- reflection and feedback reveal patterns
- a pattern is different from one isolated event

### Skills

- notice repeated responses
- compare behavior across settings
- identify common triggers
- seek examples from trusted people

### Observable Behaviors

- Records repeated reactions
- Names situations that trigger a usual response
- Compares patterns across settings
- Checks self-observations with feedback

### I Demonstrate This When

- I notice how I usually respond under pressure.
- I recognize patterns in how I communicate.
- I identify situations where I tend to struggle.
- I reflect on habits that affect my performance.

### Performance Indicators

- **Limited**: Needs prompting to demonstrate identify personal patterns and may apply it inaccurately or inconsistently.
- **Developing**: Names isolated events without seeing a pattern.
- **Effective**: Identifies a recurring pattern and its common conditions.
- **Consistent**: Uses several sources of evidence and revises conclusions when needed.

### Examples

- A learner notices they delay asking questions until a deadline is close.
- An employee sees that they become very brief when workload rises.

### Non-Examples

- Calling one mistake a pattern.
- Assuming a pattern is permanent.

### Common Misconceptions

- Patterns can be noticed without blaming yourself.
- A pattern is useful information, not a fixed identity.

### Conversation Starters

- Understanding: How can I tell whether something is a real pattern or a one-time event?
- Reflection or Application: What pattern might be affecting a situation I keep experiencing?
- Practice or Improvement: What is a simple way to track one of my patterns this week?

### FfTT-TSB 2.2 · understanding strengths and risks

The same tendency can be helpful in one situation and challenging in another. For example, being highly direct may support clarity but create tension if used without sensitivity.

### Competency Definition

Recognizing strengths and limitations

### Competency Statement

Identifies how personal tendencies can create strengths in some situations and risks or challenges in others.

### Knowledge

- strengths can be overused
- context affects whether a tendency helps
- other people may experience behavior differently
- balanced use improves effectiveness

### Skills

- name a benefit and a risk
- consider impact on others
- identify signs of overuse
- adjust the intensity of a response

### Observable Behaviors

- Describes both benefits and risks
- Notices when a strength is overused
- Invites another perspective
- Adjusts an approach when results are poor

### I Demonstrate This When

- I recognize how my strengths help me succeed.
- I notice when a strength may be overused.
- I consider how my behavior affects others.
- I adjust when my usual approach is not working.

### Performance Indicators

- **Limited**: Needs prompting to demonstrate connect strengths and risks and may apply it inaccurately or inconsistently.
- **Developing**: Sees a tendency as only good or bad.
- **Effective**: Explains both value and risk.
- **Consistent**: Anticipates overuse and adapts early.

### Examples

- Directness improves clarity but may reduce openness if tone is harsh.
- Careful analysis improves quality but may delay a time-sensitive decision.

### Non-Examples

- Avoiding a strength entirely because it once caused difficulty.
- Praising a strength without considering impact.

### Common Misconceptions

- A strength is not effective in every amount or setting.
- Managing a risk does not require rejecting the underlying strength.

### Conversation Starters

- Understanding: Why can the same tendency be a strength in one situation and a risk in another?
- Reflection or Application: When might one of my strengths be working against the outcome I want?
- Practice or Improvement: How can I adjust a strength without losing what makes it useful?

### FfTT-TSB 2.3 · avoiding trait based excuses

Traits may help explain why certain responses feel natural, but they do not remove responsibility for behavior. People can still build skills and choose more effective actions.

### Competency Definition

Building skills that improve behavior

### Competency Statement

The ability to identify, practice, and apply skills that support more effective behavior across workplace situations. Workplace Relevance Employers value people who can learn, adapt, and improve. Skill development supports communication, customer service, collaboration, leadership, emotional regulation, problem-solving, and career growth. It also helps coaching and training efforts focus on practical, observable improvement.

### Knowledge

- explanation is different from excuse
- people remain responsible for actions
- skills can help manage tendencies
- accountability includes repair and improvement

### Skills

- acknowledge impact
- accept relevant feedback
- identify a better response
- select a skill or support

### Observable Behaviors

- Owns behavior without blaming personality
- Listens to feedback
- Names an improvement action
- Follows through after a mistake

### I Demonstrate This When

- I do not use my personality as an excuse.
- I take responsibility for how I act.
- I look for skills that help me respond better.
- I accept that I can improve my behavior.

### Performance Indicators

- **Limited**: Needs prompting to demonstrate maintain accountability and may apply it inaccurately or inconsistently.
- **Developing**: Uses traits to defend behavior.
- **Effective**: Acknowledges behavior and identifies a next step.
- **Consistent**: Repairs impact and builds a plan that prevents repetition.

### Examples

- An employee says, “I tend to speak quickly, and I need to pause so others can contribute.”
- A learner apologizes for a dismissive comment and practices a better response.

### Non-Examples

- Saying, “That is just how I am.”
- Agreeing with feedback but making no change.

### Common Misconceptions

- Accountability is not self-criticism; it is ownership and action.
- Understanding a tendency can increase choice rather than reduce responsibility.

### Conversation Starters

- Understanding: How can trait awareness help me without becoming an excuse?
- Reflection or Application: How could I take responsibility in a situation where my natural reaction took over?
- Practice or Improvement: What skill could help me manage a tendency that sometimes causes problems?
- FfTT-TSB 3: Developing Skills for Behavioral Change
- Skills help people respond more effectively, even when their underlying traits remain the same. A person may still be introverted, direct, anxious, cautious, or fast-paced, but they can learn skills that improve communication, decision-making, teamwork, and self-management.
- Problem Statement
- People may believe they cannot change because their traits feel stable, or they may expect change without practicing the skills needed to behave differently.
- Value Statement
- Skill development gives people practical tools to improve behavior, adapt to situations, and strengthen workplace performance.

## Competency 3 · Developing Skills for Behavioral Change

### FfTT-TSB 3.1 · identifying skill gaps

Before people can change behavior, they need to understand which skills are missing or underdeveloped. This helps development planning become more specific and useful.

### Competency Definition

Recognizing skills needed for improvement

### Competency Statement

Identifies skills that need to be developed in order to improve workplace behavior, communication, performance, or relationships.

### Knowledge

- a behavior problem may have several causes
- skill gaps should be specific
- feedback and evidence improve accuracy
- priority depends on impact and opportunity

### Skills

- connect behavior to a skill
- gather evidence
- choose a high-value gap
- write a specific development need

### Observable Behaviors

- Names a specific skill
- Uses examples to support the need
- Checks whether barriers are also present
- Prioritizes one or two skills

### I Demonstrate This When

- I identify the skills I need to improve.
- I connect behavior challenges to skill development.
- I ask for feedback about where I can grow.
- I focus on specific skills rather than vague improvement goals.

### Performance Indicators

- **Limited**: Needs prompting to demonstrate identify skill gaps and may apply it inaccurately or inconsistently.
- **Developing**: Uses vague goals.
- **Effective**: Links a recurring challenge to a specific skill.
- **Consistent**: Distinguishes skill gaps from unclear expectations or missing resources.

### Examples

- A worker links repeated customer misunderstandings to questioning and summarizing skills.
- A student identifies planning as the skill needed to start assignments earlier.

### Non-Examples

- Calling every performance problem a motivation issue.
- Selecting many skills without priorities.

### Common Misconceptions

- Not every problem is a skill gap; resources, expectations, or workload may matter.
- A skill gap is a development target, not a personal flaw.

### Conversation Starters

- Understanding: How can I tell whether a problem comes from a skill gap or something else?
- Reflection or Application: What skill may be missing in a situation where I am not getting the result I want?
- Practice or Improvement: How should I choose which skill gap to work on first?

### FfTT-TSB 3.2 · practicing new responses

Behavioral change requires practice. People need opportunities to rehearse new responses before they can use them consistently in real workplace situations.

### Competency Definition

Practicing alternative workplace responses

### Competency Statement

Practices new communication, self-management, decision-making, or interpersonal responses to support more effective behavior.

### Knowledge

- practice should target a clear action
- realistic scenarios improve transfer
- feedback guides adjustment
- repetition supports confidence and fluency

### Skills

- rehearse a response
- use role play or simulation
- request focused feedback
- repeat with increasing difficulty

### Observable Behaviors

- Practices a specific action
- Uses realistic examples
- Adjusts after feedback
- Repeats practice across conditions

### I Demonstrate This When

- I practice a better response before I need it.
- I try new ways of handling difficult situations.
- I use coaching or feedback to improve.
- I keep practicing until I become more confident.

### Performance Indicators

- **Limited**: Needs prompting to demonstrate practice new responses and may apply it inaccurately or inconsistently.
- **Developing**: Talks about change without rehearsal.
- **Effective**: Practices a clear response and improves it.
- **Consistent**: Uses varied scenarios and can explain what changed.

### Examples

- A learner role-plays how to ask for clarification from a supervisor.
- A team member rehearses a calm opening for a difficult conversation.

### Non-Examples

- Memorizing a script without adapting to the listener.
- Practicing only after a serious problem occurs.

### Common Misconceptions

- Practice is not fake; it prepares a person to act intentionally.
- Early practice may feel awkward and still be useful.

### Conversation Starters

- Understanding: Why does practicing a response help when I already know what I should do?
- Reflection or Application: What situation could I rehearse before I face it again?
- Practice or Improvement: Can you help me practice a better response and give focused feedback?

### FfTT-TSB 3.3 · translating learning into action

Knowing what to do is not the same as doing it. Learners must apply skills in real situations and adjust based on results.

### Competency Definition

Interpreting behavior within situations

### Competency Statement

The ability to understand how situations influence behavior and to interpret actions fairly, accurately, and constructively. Workplace Relevance Workplaces are complex environments where behavior is affected by deadlines, customers, supervisors, team dynamics, workload, stress, culture, and expectations. Employees and leaders who understand context are better able to coach, communicate, resolve conflict, and improve performance without making unfair assumptions.

### Knowledge

- transfer requires attention and reminders
- results provide evidence
- setbacks are part of learning
- consistent use builds habits

### Skills

- choose an application opportunity
- use a cue or reminder
- review the outcome
- adjust the next attempt

### Observable Behaviors

- Uses the skill in a relevant setting
- Notes what happened
- Requests feedback when useful
- Changes the plan based on evidence

### I Demonstrate This When

- I use what I learned in real situations.
- I notice whether my new approach is working.
- I adjust when I need to improve further.
- I turn learning into consistent action.

### Performance Indicators

- **Limited**: Needs prompting to demonstrate apply learning in real situations and may apply it inaccurately or inconsistently.
- **Developing**: Keeps learning separate from work.
- **Effective**: Applies and reviews a skill.
- **Consistent**: Uses the skill flexibly across situations and sustains it.

### Examples

- After practicing active listening, an employee summarizes a coworker’s concern in a real meeting.
- A learner uses a pause strategy during an actual disagreement and reviews the result.

### Non-Examples

- Waiting for a perfect opportunity.
- Assuming one successful attempt means the behavior is established.

### Common Misconceptions

- Knowing a method does not ensure transfer.
- A difficult attempt can still produce useful learning.

### Conversation Starters

- Understanding: What makes it hard to use a skill in a real situation even after I practice it?
- Reflection or Application: Where could I apply a new skill in my current work or learning?
- Practice or Improvement: What reminder or review method could help me turn this skill into a habit?
- FfTT-TSB 4: Understanding Behavior in Context
- Behavior is shaped by the situation. A person may behave differently depending on pressure, expectations, relationships, culture, environment, role, incentives, confidence, or support. Understanding context helps people avoid unfair judgments and respond more effectively.
- Problem Statement
- People often judge behavior without considering the situation, which can lead to inaccurate conclusions, unfair labels, and missed opportunities for improvement.
- Value Statement
- Understanding context improves fairness, communication, coaching, teamwork, and problem-solving.

## Competency 4 · Understanding Behavior in Context

### FfTT-TSB 4.1 · recognizing situational influences

Situations can strongly affect behavior. Recognizing these influences helps people understand why behavior may change across settings.

### Competency Definition

Recognizing how context affects behavior

### Competency Statement

Identifies situational factors that may influence how a person behaves in a specific moment or environment.

### Knowledge

- context can enable or constrain behavior
- power and role affect communication
- stress and uncertainty affect responses
- culture influences expectations

### Skills

- scan the situation
- identify pressures and supports
- consider role and power
- ask what changed

### Observable Behaviors

- Names relevant conditions
- Considers both personal and situational factors
- Checks assumptions
- Avoids using context to erase impact

### I Demonstrate This When

- I consider what was happening in the situation.
- I recognize that pressure can affect behavior.
- I think about expectations before judging actions.
- I look beyond the behavior alone.

### Performance Indicators

- **Limited**: Needs prompting to demonstrate recognize situational influences and may apply it inaccurately or inconsistently.
- **Developing**: Ignores context.
- **Effective**: Identifies several plausible influences.
- **Consistent**: Weighs evidence and keeps accountability in view.

### Examples

- A quiet employee speaks less when senior leaders enter the room.
- A normally organized worker misses steps during an unexpected system outage.

### Non-Examples

- Assuming context excuses harmful behavior.
- Inventing a situational explanation without evidence.

### Common Misconceptions

- Context helps explain behavior but does not automatically justify it.
- The same context may affect people differently.

### Conversation Starters

- Understanding: What kinds of situational factors can change how someone behaves?
- Reflection or Application: What context may have shaped a recent behavior that surprised me?
- Practice or Improvement: How can I ask about context without making excuses for the behavior?

### FfTT-TSB 4.2 · interpreting behavior carefully

One behavior does not define a person. Careful interpretation helps people avoid overgeneralizing from a single moment or interaction.

### Competency Definition

Avoiding quick behavioral judgments

### Competency Statement

Interprets behavior carefully by focusing on observable actions, context, patterns, and impact rather than unsupported assumptions.

### Knowledge

- one event provides limited evidence
- facts and assumptions differ
- patterns need repeated evidence
- impact can be addressed without proving intent

### Skills

- state observations
- identify assumptions
- look for patterns
- ask clarifying questions

### Observable Behaviors

- Uses neutral descriptions
- Names uncertainty
- Checks for repeated behavior
- Focuses on impact and expectations

### I Demonstrate This When

- I avoid judging someone based on one moment.
- I separate what I saw from what I assume.
- I look for patterns before drawing conclusions.
- I describe impact without labeling the person.

### Performance Indicators

- **Limited**: Needs prompting to demonstrate interpret behavior carefully and may apply it inaccurately or inconsistently.
- **Developing**: Makes a quick character judgment.
- **Effective**: Uses evidence and clarifies uncertainty.
- **Consistent**: Updates an interpretation when new information appears.

### Examples

- A supervisor asks why a report was late before concluding the employee is careless.
- A peer describes a comment and its impact without claiming to know the speaker’s motive.

### Non-Examples

- Ignoring repeated evidence in the name of being fair.
- Treating a guess about intent as fact.

### Common Misconceptions

- Careful interpretation is not avoidance; concerns can still be addressed.
- Intent and impact are related but not identical.

### Conversation Starters

- Understanding: Why is one behavior usually not enough to judge a person?
- Reflection or Application: Where might I be mixing an observation with an assumption?
- Practice or Improvement: How can I address impact when I am unsure about someone’s intent?

### FfTT-TSB 4.3 · adjusting behavior to fit the situation

Effective workplace behavior often requires adapting to the situation. This does not mean being fake. It means choosing actions that fit the context, audience, and expectations.

### Competency Definition

Managing tendencies through intentional behavior

### Competency Statement

The ability to manage personal tendencies, emotions, impulses, and reactions in order to behave professionally across workplace situations. Workplace Relevance Professional behavior is essential for employability, customer service, teamwork, supervision, leadership, and workplace trust. Employees who manage themselves well are more reliable, coachable, adaptable, and effective under pressure.

### Knowledge

- effective behavior depends on purpose and audience
- adaptation is not dishonesty
- safety and ethics set boundaries
- timing and tone affect impact

### Skills

- assess audience and purpose
- change tone or detail
- select timing and channel
- maintain values while adapting

### Observable Behaviors

- Adjusts communication
- Explains the reason for adaptation
- Keeps behavior respectful
- Does not adapt to harmful or unethical pressure

### I Demonstrate This When

- I adjust my approach based on the situation.
- I consider who I am speaking with.
- I choose the right tone and timing.
- I act in ways that support the outcome I want.

### Performance Indicators

- **Limited**: Needs prompting to demonstrate adapt behavior to the situation and may apply it inaccurately or inconsistently.
- **Developing**: Uses one style everywhere.
- **Effective**: Adapts appropriately to clear needs.
- **Consistent**: Balances flexibility, authenticity, inclusion, and boundaries.

### Examples

- A technical expert uses plain language with a community audience.
- A team member postpones sensitive feedback until a private setting.

### Non-Examples

- Changing facts to please an audience.
- Following an unfair norm that harms others.

### Common Misconceptions

- Authenticity does not require acting the same in every setting.
- Adaptation should not require accepting unsafe or discriminatory treatment.

### Conversation Starters

- Understanding: How is adapting my behavior different from being fake?
- Reflection or Application: What part of my approach might need to change for a current audience or situation?
- Practice or Improvement: How can I adapt while still staying true to my values and boundaries?
- FfTT-TSB 5: Managing Traits Through Professional Behavior
- Traits can influence natural reactions, but professional behavior requires self-management. People may need to manage impatience, anxiety, directness, sensitivity, risk-aversion, impulsiveness, or low confidence so they can respond effectively at work.
- Problem Statement
- When people do not manage their natural tendencies, they may react impulsively, avoid responsibility, damage relationships, or behave in ways that reduce trust.
- Value Statement
- Professional self-management helps people use their tendencies constructively while meeting workplace expectations.

## Competency 5 · Managing Traits Through Professional Behavior

### FfTT-TSB 5.1 · managing emotional responses

Emotions affect behavior, especially during stress, conflict, feedback, or uncertainty. Emotional management helps people stay professional even when they feel challenged.

### Competency Definition

Regulating emotions professionally

### Competency Statement

Manages emotional responses in order to remain calm, respectful, focused, and effective during workplace situations.

### Knowledge

- emotions provide information
- regulation is different from suppression
- triggers and body signals can be noticed
- strategies vary by person and situation

### Skills

- name an emotion
- notice early signals
- use a calming or grounding strategy
- choose a professional response

### Observable Behaviors

- Recognizes rising emotion
- Uses a healthy strategy
- Maintains respect
- Seeks support when needed

### I Demonstrate This When

- I stay calm when I feel stressed.
- I manage frustration before responding.
- I remain respectful during difficult conversations.
- I avoid letting emotions control my behavior.

### Performance Indicators

- **Limited**: Needs prompting to demonstrate manage emotional responses and may apply it inaccurately or inconsistently.
- **Developing**: Denies or releases emotion without control.
- **Effective**: Uses a strategy and remains effective.
- **Consistent**: Anticipates triggers and selects strategies flexibly.

### Examples

- A learner notices anxiety and uses slow breathing before a presentation.

### Non-Examples

- Pretending not to feel anything.
- Using anger as permission to insult someone.

### Common Misconceptions

- Managing emotion does not mean never feeling it.
- Stepping away can be responsible when done safely and communicated clearly.

### Conversation Starters

- Reflection or Application: What early signs tell me that an emotion may affect my behavior?
- Practice or Improvement: Which strategies could I practice for staying professional when emotions are strong?

- use a pause cue
- consider options
- select a response

### Observable Behaviors

- Uses a visible or private cue
- Considers impact
